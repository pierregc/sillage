# Old stars seen edge-on and face-on

A viewer, on one galaxy turned through ninety degrees: edge-on a wide yellow cloud stands above
and below the disk, face-on the yellow is gone, core included (#163).

**Some of that is projection, and right.** A bulge or a halo is a spheroid, so its surface
brightness is the same from every side. Edge-on the disk collapses into a thin lane and the
spheroid is all there is off the plane; face-on it lies under a disk that outshines it. Real
galaxies do this: the bulge of NGC 4565 is obvious edge-on, the one of M101 is a small warm
nucleus face-on. A bulge carrying 4.5 % of the light, as the isolated disk's does, owns only the
inner half kiloparsec face-on (87 % of the light there) and is drowned beyond.

**The cloud was the outskirts.** Thick disk and inner halo, 10 % of the particles, 1.7 % of the
light, but 50 % of what lies more than a kiloparsec off the plane edge-on, and they puff up over
a run. A stellar halo holds well under one per cent of a galaxy's light and does not show in an
ordinary image from any angle. `outskirtLight` takes them to 0.5 %, and 23 % of the off-plane
light. Face-on the radial profile does not move: the soft edge they give the disk survives.

**The core was white because it was young.** Away from the arms the sampler's arm proximity is a
neutral 0.5, and the arm term drew `0.40 * 0.5` of the stars as arm stars of 60 to 900 Myr,
inside the bulge too, where no arm reaches. Light-weighted, the disk stars inside 0.5 kpc came
out at 8400 K. Weighted by `1 - bulgeWeight` they are old there, 4650 K. Neither change takes a
random draw, so every scene keeps its particles and only their light moves.

**Left open.** Over a run the gas just outside the bulge turns into stars, and the young knots
make the core pink-white again by 300 Myr. Leaving the bulge's stars out of the star formation
density changed nothing measurable, so it is not the bulge's density that drives it.
